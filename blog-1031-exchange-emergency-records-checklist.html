Storm-Proof Your 1031 Exchange File Before the Clock Gets Tight

September 18, 2026   |   Stephanie Strack   |   1085 words

A 1031 exchange already runs on a strict calendar. Add a Gulf Coast storm, a closed office, a damaged phone, or a missing document, and a manageable transaction can become unnecessarily hard to control.

September is National Preparedness Month, and the IRS is encouraging taxpayers to update emergency plans and protect important records. For Texas landowners in an exchange, that advice is especially practical. You do not need a storm in the forecast to build a better file. You need the file ready before a disruption tests it.

Put the two federal deadlines at the top

For a typical deferred exchange, the IRS instructions for Form 8824 describe two dates that deserve constant visibility:

  • The replacement property generally must be identified within 45 days after the transfer of the relinquished property.
  • The replacement property generally must be received by the earlier of 180 days after that transfer or the due date, including extensions, of the tax return for the year of the transfer.

These periods run concurrently. They are not two separate clocks that begin one after the other.

There are detailed rules for how replacement property is identified, who receives the identification, and what happens when multiple properties are named. A qualified intermediary, CPA, and attorney should guide the exchange structure and documents. The practical point for the owner is simple: know the original transfer date, know the resulting deadlines, and preserve proof of every time-sensitive instruction.

Do not assume a hurricane, office closure, or local emergency automatically pauses an exchange deadline. The IRS may grant disaster relief for specified events, locations, taxpayers, and periods, but relief depends on the official notice and the facts. Your professional advisers should confirm whether any published relief actually applies.

Build one storm-ready exchange record

A useful exchange file should be accessible to the right people even if one office, device, or person is unavailable. Keep secure electronic copies and, where appropriate, protected paper copies of the following:

  1. The relinquished-property contract, settlement statement, deed, and transfer date.
  2. The qualified-intermediary agreement and current contact information.
  3. The written replacement-property identification and proof of timely delivery.
  4. Purchase contracts, amendments, inspection reports, surveys, title commitments, and environmental or access material for candidate properties.
  5. Lender, title company, insurer, attorney, CPA, agent, and property-contact details.
  6. A one-page deadline sheet showing the transfer date, day 45, day 180, tax-return due date, and the person responsible for each next action.
  7. A log of material calls, decisions, notices, and document deliveries.

Store sensitive records in a secure system with access controls. A shared inbox with no ownership is not a disaster plan. Name a primary record keeper and a backup who knows where the file lives and how to reach the team.

Make the identification proof easy to find

The IRS instructions require replacement-property identification to be made in writing, clearly describe the property, and be signed by the taxpayer. The written identification must be delivered by the deadline to a person permitted under the rules, not simply kept in a private note.

Keep the final signed identification together with evidence of delivery and receipt. If the description relies on a legal description, street address, parcel information, or other material, preserve the exact version that was transmitted. Drafts should be labeled as drafts so no one confuses them with the operative notice.

This is one of those places where tidy records matter. After a disruption, the question should not be, “Which attachment did we send?” It should be answered by the file.

Protect due diligence from a weather interruption

A storm can affect site access, inspections, surveys, utilities, title work, insurance availability, lending, and contractor schedules. Build some margin into the process instead of planning every task for the last available day.

For each candidate replacement property, track:

  • Who can authorize access if the owner or agent is unavailable.
  • Whether roads, gates, bridges, low-water crossings, or tenants may limit post-storm access.
  • Which inspections or reports are still outstanding.
  • Whether the insurer or lender needs updated information after a weather event.
  • Who will communicate a delay, and to whom.
  • Which decision can move forward with the information already in hand.

Land transactions have their own field realities. Water, drainage, fencing, access, agricultural use, minerals, leases, and improvements may all require documents or site review. A secure digital file does not replace boots-on-the-ground diligence, but it keeps the team from losing time rebuilding the record.

Make a contact sheet that works without one phone

The exchange team often spans several businesses. Put the direct number and backup contact for the qualified intermediary, closing office, CPA, attorney, lender, insurer, agent, surveyor, inspector, and seller-side contact on one page.

Include the transaction or file number each party uses. Note normal business hours and an approved backup communication method. Keep the sheet with the deadline summary, but do not add passwords or confidential access codes.

Then test it. Ask one person who is not managing the exchange day to day to find the current identification document, deadline sheet, and qualified-intermediary contact. If it takes more than a few minutes, simplify the file.

Check official relief, not social media summaries

When the IRS announces disaster relief, it publishes the covered area, eligible taxpayers, postponed acts, and applicable dates. Relief can be highly specific. A general news story or a friend's experience in another county is not enough to determine whether your transaction qualifies.

If a disaster affects the deal, save the relevant official notice and the written analysis from your tax or legal adviser. Continue moving the transaction forward where safely possible until your advisers confirm a different course.

A calm file supports a better land decision

The goal of an emergency records plan is not paperwork for paperwork's sake. It protects decision time. When the core documents, dates, contacts, and due-diligence status are clear, an investor can spend more attention evaluating the replacement property itself.
