Selling Ranch Land in Wharton County: What Should Be Ready Before Listing?

September 2, 2026   |   Stephanie Strack   |   1027 words

Before listing ranch land in Wharton County, get the property records and the ground telling the same story. Gather the survey, access documents, current tax information, leases, and water-system records. Then walk the ranch and decide what needs attention before a buyer arrives.

A good-looking entrance helps. A clear answer about where the boundary runs, how the well has been maintained, or who has grazing rights helps a buyer understand what they are being asked to purchase.

For a landowner around El Campo, Wharton, or East Bernard, the useful first step is a seller preparation folder. Here is what belongs in it.

Put the boundary and access records together

Start with the deed and legal description, any existing survey, and the property account information. Keep them together so your agent and title company can spot questions early. If ownership is held through an estate, trust, or business, identify who has authority to sign and have the appropriate professionals review the supporting documents.

Mark the entrance used today and gather any recorded access easement or shared-road agreement. Note who maintains the road and whether another person uses the same gate. A well-worn road and a recorded right to use it are different things.

During the property walk, flag fence corners that do not appear to match the survey. Do not redraw a boundary from an aerial image or advertise a disputed line as settled. Ask a licensed surveyor and the title company what needs clarification.

Document what stays, what leaves, and who uses the land

Write down the improvements and equipment you expect to include. Separate permanent improvements from movable items such as feeders, panels, portable buildings, and equipment. Identify anything leased or owned by someone else.

Gather grazing, farming, hunting, residential, and other agreements affecting the property. Include written contracts and a description of any verbal arrangements. Record the parties, dates, payment terms, and practical access needs for review.

The Texas Real Estate Commission's contract library is the source for current farm-and-ranch forms and related addenda. Have your agent and, when needed, your attorney address the actual transaction. This preparation folder gives them facts to work with.

Show the agricultural appraisal records, not a promise

Include the latest tax statement, appraisal notice, and records supporting the property's agricultural or wildlife-management use. Note which acreage is covered and whether homesites or other areas are treated differently.

What people often call an ag exemption is generally a special appraisal based on productivity. It is not a promise that the next owner's taxes will stay the same. The Texas Comptroller's agricultural appraisal guidance explains qualification and changes in use.

Before making a claim in the listing, confirm the current record with the appraisal district. Let the buyer investigate the requirements for their intended use. Keep grazing records, leases, and other supporting documents available rather than relying on a remembered tax bill.

Make the water and septic information usable

For each well, gather the drilling report if available, pump-service invoices, recent testing, and any applicable registration or permit records. Identify which well serves the house, livestock, or other improvements. Note known problems honestly.

If the drilling report is missing, the Texas Water Development Board's well-report guide explains where to search. An approximate drilling date and the owner's name at that time can help locate the record.

A drilling report is historical information. It does not establish today's water quality or guarantee current production. Buyers may need their own inspections and testing.

For septic systems, collect the available permits, service records, and system-location information. Do not describe a system as approved, sized for expansion, or recently inspected unless the records support that statement.

Check drainage and development questions before advertising possibilities

Take notes on low areas, drainage routes, creek crossings, and places that become difficult to reach after rain. Include known flooding history and available elevation or drainage documents in the review packet.

Wharton County's Permits and Inspections page provides local development, septic, drainage, and floodplain resources. Use those records and the appropriate local office when checking a proposed use. Requirements can depend on the tract's location and the work proposed.

Avoid calling land ready for a homesite, subdivision, or commercial project because it looks open on a map. Describe what has been verified and identify questions that remain.

Walk it once for repairs, then again for the showing

Make a short list of broken gates, loose wire, unsafe debris, obstructed access, and water leaks. Separate necessary safety work from appearance improvements and larger projects that need a cost discussion.

Before spending on major clearing or new infrastructure, talk through the property's likely buyer and the purpose of the work. There is no universal repair list that guarantees a higher sale price.

Plan a showing route that explains the ranch. Note where visitors can park, which gates must stay closed, where livestock are located, and which areas should be avoided. Keep gate codes and security details out of public marketing.

Common Questions

Do I need a new survey before I list?

Start by finding the existing survey. Your agent, title company, and surveyor can help determine whether an update or new survey is appropriate. Do not assume an older survey will be accepted for every transaction.

Should I fix every fence before selling?

Start with safety and function. Then compare the proposed work with the property's intended market. A documented condition and a clear repair discussion can be more useful than spending without a plan.

What should I bring to the first listing conversation?

Bring the records you have, the questions you cannot answer yet, and a short list of your family's priorities. Missing records can become an organized work list.
